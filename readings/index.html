Readings

Week 2

Carrión's line that mail art “knocks at the door of the castle where the Big Monster lives” suggests the work truly comes alive through the postal system's frictions—bureaucracy, delays, stamps, even errors. Misdeliveries and slow arrivals become part of the piece, making the Monster a quiet collaborator rather than a barrier.

  • Does the artist share authorship with the postal system if delivery errors influence meaning?
  • If errors and delays are embraced, does chance blur the boundary between deliberate creation and accident?
Week 3

Schwulst's metaphors of websites as gardens, puddles, or shelves reframe sites as living spaces—ongoing processes, not static products. Édouard U's “knowledge networks” extend this idea: meaning arises from connection rather than isolation. Together they argue for mindful, cultivated web-making.

  • If websites are living spaces that change over time, how should we rethink permanence and preservation on the web?
  • Could “surfing, not searching” resist algorithmic funnels on platforms like Instagram or TikTok?
Week 4

Lialina reads Geocities as digital folklore. The missing images, broken links, and “under construction” banners are historical traces, not failures. Those ruins document how people experimented with self‑expression and shape our memory of an early, more open web.

  • What is lost when platforms 'clean up' user design in favor of standardized templates?
  • Can broken or unfinished websites hold the same cultural value as preserved ones?
Week 5

They propose a post‑digital ecology where print and digital overlap. DIY zines, crowdfunding, and computer‑generated books merge print's tactile presence with digital connectivity and remix culture, reframing publishing as hybrid cultural creation.

  • Do hybrids like zines with QR codes expand print's possibilities or dilute its uniqueness?
  • How do ownership and sharing politics shift in hybrid, networked publishing?
Week 7

The workbook reframes accessibility as creative practice. Treating alt‑text as translation and poetry spotlights how description shapes perception, turning accessibility into an empathetic, aesthetic act that expands interpretive space for creators and audiences.

  • How does viewing alt‑text as poetic change our approach to accessibility in design?
  • If alt‑text always involves creative decisions, can it be objective—and should that be the goal?
Week 8

Nasser shows how ‘universal' computing is built on English and Western defaults. His Arabic language, Qalb, exposes how tools, encodings, and naming conventions embed cultural bias, challenging the possibility of neutral programming.

  • Why does Nasser see programming as culturally biased instead of purely technical?
  • Can programming languages work equally well for speakers of all languages?
Week 9

Reinfurt ties Gestalt theory to design practice: people group parts into meaningful wholes. Using proximity, similarity, closure, and continuity, designers align with perception to guide attention and make communication clear.

  • How does Gestalt theory help designers make work easier to understand?
  • What are examples of Gestalt ideas we see in everyday design?
Week 10

Duvall charts a move into hypermodernism: infinite digital surfaces and hyperlinked signs. Graphic design becomes the architecture of the internet, structuring how meanings multiply, connect, and compress online.

  • What does “hypermodernism” mean here, and how does it differ from postmodernism?
  • How has the internet changed how we use and read signs in graphic design?
Week 12

Tuer reads Vera Frenkel's work as a poetics of absence. String Games turns telepresence into both bridge and barrier; Body Missing uses hypertext to remember stolen art and lost lives. Technology preserves and erases—memory lives in that tension.

  • What in Frenkel's art makes us think differently about technology in new media?
  • How do String Games and Body Missing show tech can both keep and erase memories?
